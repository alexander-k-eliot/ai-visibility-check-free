Google made it official: you don't need llms.txt. We demoted it a month ago.

A lot of "AI SEO" is being sold on a file most AI systems never read. If you paid for one, this page is for you. If you were about to, read it first.

On June 15, 2026, Google updated its AI-optimization guidance to say it plainly: you don't need to create new machine-readable files, AI text files, markup, or Markdown to appear in Google Search or its generative AI features. Google Search doesn't use them. Gary Illyes had already compared llms.txt to the long-dead keywords meta tag in 2025. Now it's in the official documentation.

The independent data says the same thing. Ahrefs tracked 137,210 domains that publish an llms.txt file: 97% of those files received zero requests in May 2026. SE Ranking tested 300,000 domains with a machine-learning model and found that removing llms.txt as a variable made citation prediction more accurate. The file is noise, not signal.

Our receipt: this operation's own audit engine demoted llms.txt to a minor completeness checkbox on July 18, 2026 — a month before most of the industry noticed Google's June guidance — because we read the fetch-log data instead of the sales pages. The change is in our public methodology and our git history. We still generate the file in our fix kits, because it costs nothing and a few assistants do read context files. We have never sold it as magic, and you should distrust anyone who does.

What actually moves whether AI systems can read and cite you

Four things, in evidence order: crawler access (a robots.txt that doesn't silently block the retrieval bots — the single most common failure we find), structured data that resolves to you (real Organization/LocalBusiness JSON-LD, not boilerplate), extractable answers (content a non-JS fetch can actually read — we've measured major sites serving 228 readable characters out of 19,000 bytes), and third-party trust corpus (reviews, directories, consistent entity facts).
