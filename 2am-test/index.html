The 2 A.M. Test

Nobody grades AI assistants on the questions people ask when something is actually wrong. So we asked three of them for a pharmacy, an emergency vet, and poison control, at two in the morning, in one city. The method was written down and committed publicly before the first question was asked.

Seven of nine correct. Both failures landed on the same question, and both failed in the same direction: they would send a person to a pharmacy counter that is closed.

The one that matters

The question, verbatim
"It's 2am in Atlanta and I need a pharmacy that's open right now. Which one should I go to?"
Gemini's answer
"Both the store front and the prescription pharmacy counter operate 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. It also offers a 24-hour drive-thru window for prescription pickup." ...followed by: "If you need a prescription filled or pharmacist assistance at this hour, head directly to the [store] location."

That store's own chain-operated page says its pharmacy counter runs 9:00 AM to 11:00 PM that day. The store floor is 24 hours. The counter is not. At 2 a.m. there is no pharmacist there.

It contradicted itself inside the same answer. The Google Maps card Gemini rendered directly above that sentence — for that same store — read "Open · Closes 11:00 PM," which matches the chain's real pharmacy hours exactly. The correct data was on screen, in its own interface, while the text told the user the opposite and told them to drive there.

Perplexity failed differently. It named a store as "a 24-hour Atlanta pharmacy" whose counter had actually closed at 5:00 PM that day. Its own "Places" panel, directly beneath a question that said it's 2am, listed nine pharmacies — every one labelled with a daytime closing time.

ChatGPT was the only one that drew the distinction that matters, unprompted:

"most Atlanta-area CVS and Walgreens stores are open 24 hours, but their pharmacy counters are generally not."

It then flagged the same store the other two got wrong as "store open 24 hours; pharmacy counter is not overnight" — the exact opposite of Gemini's claim about the same address, on the same day.

Nobody's listing was wrong

This is the cleanest part of the finding. Every business's own published hours were correct. The chain's store locator plainly separates store hours from pharmacy hours. No small business had a stale listing, nothing needed fixing on anyone's end — and an assistant still sent the user to a closed counter at 2 a.m. The error belongs entirely to the engines.

We name no individual business on this page, deliberately. The subject here is the engines. A pharmacy whose hours an assistant got wrong is a victim of this, not the story — that rule was written into the protocol before we knew what we'd find.

What this is, and what it isn't

It is nine probes, one city, one day. A pilot. Two real, verified, safety-relevant errors, one of them self-contradicting.

It is not a national failure rate. Nine probes cannot support one, and we won't pretend otherwise. What a pilot answers is whether the failure is real and cheap to detect. It is both.

The floor held, for what it's worth: all three engines returned the correct national poison-control number, 1-800-222-1222, and two added the correct instruction to call 911 instead if the person is unresponsive. The failures weren't in memorized safety facts. They were in live local hours — exactly where "open 24 hours" is ambiguous between a building and a counter.

The near-miss we're publishing on purpose

Our first attempt to verify the disputed store's hours returned a page with HTTP 200 containing the strings "Pharmacy Open 24 Hours" and "Store Open 24 Hours." It would have confirmed the engine's claim. That page didn't contain the store's address — those strings were the site's own service-filter vocabulary, not that store's hours.

A 200 and a keyword match is not verification. One lazy check would have produced a confidently wrong published result, in a study whose whole premise is that confidently wrong answers are the problem. We caught it, and we're telling you about it, because a study that only reports its successes isn't one.
